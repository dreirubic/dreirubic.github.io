1. Understanding and Doing Science

Science is a way of asking questions about the world and answering them with evidence, not guesses. Doing science usually follows this cycle:

Observe → notice something using your senses or tools (a liquid is cloudy, a rock feels heavy).
Measure → describe it with numbers and units (volume in mL, mass in g).
Classify → group it based on shared properties.
Investigate → test an idea (hypothesis) through a fair experiment.
Conclude → decide what the evidence shows, and be ready to revise if new evidence appears.

Real-life example: A student notices sugar "disappears" in water. She measures 10 g of sugar and 100 mL of water, stirs, and observes the mixture stays clear — this is an investigation, not just a guess.

Why measurement matters

A good measurement always has a number and a unit. "The beaker has water" is an observation; "the beaker has 250 mL of water" is a measurement. Common Grade 7 lab units: mass (g, kg), volume (mL, L), length (cm, m), temperature (°C).

2. Pure Substances vs. Mixtures

Pure Substance

Made of only one kind of matter, with a fixed composition and fixed properties (constant melting/boiling point). Cannot be separated by simple physical means.

Mixture

Made of two or more substances combined physically, in a composition that can vary. Can be separated by physical methods (filtering, evaporation, sifting).

Elements vs. Compounds (both are pure substances)

Element the simplest pure substance — one type of atom only. Cannot be broken down further by ordinary chemical means. Examples: oxygen (O₂), iron (Fe), gold (Au), carbon (C).

Compound two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio, forming a new substance with new properties. Can only be broken apart by a chemical reaction. Examples: water (H₂O), table salt (NaCl), carbon dioxide (CO₂).

3. Homogeneous Mixtures / Solutions

Solution a homogeneous mixture — its components are blended so evenly that you cannot see the separate parts, even under a normal microscope. One substance (the solute) dissolves in another (the solvent, usually the one in larger amount).

Examples: salt water (salt = solute, water = solvent), soft drinks, air (a solution of gases), vinegar, brewed tea without pulp.
